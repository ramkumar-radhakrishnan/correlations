What the exponential integrals are telling you

Why $\mathrm{Ei}$ blows up at $\Lambda\to0$ and at $y'\to z$, and which of the two is a divergence

$\kappa\equiv k\cdot(y'-z)$,  $\lambda\equiv\Lambda/k^{+}$,   $\Xi\equiv(\vee-k^{+})/k^{+}$,  $\ell_{k}\equiv\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$

1. Why it blows up in both limits: $\mathrm{Ei}$ only sees the product

Your $p^{+}$ integral produces $\mathrm{Ei}(-i\kappa\lambda)$, and for small argument

$$\mathrm{Ei}(-iu)=\gamma_{E}+\log(iu)+O(u).$$

The argument is the product $u=\kappa\lambda$. Mathematica cannot tell you whether it became small because $\Lambda\to0$ or because $y'\to z$ — it is the same number:

   kappa=1      lam=1e-06   product=1e-06:  Ei = -13.23829489 - 1.570797j
   kappa=0.001  lam=0.001   product=1e-06:  Ei = -13.23829489 - 1.570797j
   kappa=1e-06  lam=1       product=1e-06:  Ei = -13.23829489 - 1.570797j
   kappa=2      lam=5e-07   product=1e-06:  Ei = -13.23829489 - 1.570797j

So the blow–up is one logarithm, which splits as

$$\log(i\kappa\lambda)=\underbrace{\log\lambda}_{=\,-\ell_{k}}\;+\;\underbrace{\log\big(i\,k\cdot(y'-z)\big)}_{\text{a function of the transverse variables}}.$$

These two are completely different objects. The first is the rapidity divergence — real, and the thing you subtract. The second is a logarithmic singularity of the integrand at a point, which is a different animal from a divergence of the integral.

2. The $y'\to z$ piece is not a transverse divergence

A logarithm is integrable in two dimensions. $\log(y'-z)^{2}$ is also “undefined” at $y'=z$, yet $\int d^{2}(y'-z)\,\log(y'-z)^{2}$ is perfectly finite. Here the integrand near $y'=z$ is $\frac{1}{\rho}\times\log\rho$ and

$$\int_{0}\rho\,d\rho\,\frac{\log\rho}{\rho}=\int_{0}d\rho\,\log\rho\;<\;\infty.$$

Measured — the disc integral of the $p^{+}$–integrated integrand, the one with the $\mathrm{Ei}$'s in it:

   |int_{|y'-z| < 1e-01} d^2y'| = 9.2229e-04
   |int_{|y'-z| < 1e-02} d^2y'| = 1.0765e-05    ratio 0.0117
   |int_{|y'-z| < 1e-03} d^2y'| = 1.0785e-07    ratio 0.0100

$0.01$ per decade means $F\propto\epsilon^{2}$ — it does better than merely converging, because the leading $\frac{r^{m}}{r^{2}}\log|r|$ still averages to zero over angles.

So: no. The $\mathrm{Ei}$ being undefined at $y'=z$ does not mean a transverse divergence, and it does not obstruct the $+$ prescription. It is the rapidity divergence wearing a transverse disguise, because $\mathrm{Ei}$ reports only $\kappa\lambda$.

3. But the same logarithm does bite — at the other end

Take $\vee\to\infty$ properly. $\mathrm{Ei}(-i\kappa\Xi)\to-i\pi\,{\rm sgn}\,\kappa$ (a constant, not zero), so the bracket becomes

$$-\big[\mathrm{Ei}(-i\kappa\Xi)-\mathrm{Ei}(-i\kappa\lambda)\big] \;\longrightarrow\;-\ell_{k}+\gamma_{E}+\log\big(i\,k\cdot(y'-z)\big),$$

which grows like $\log|y'-z|$ at large separation, while the kernel structure falls only as $1/r^{2}$ with a nonvanishing angular average ($\rho^{2}\langle T_{2}\rangle=+0.028221$, constant from $\rho=10$ to $10^{3}$, matching $-\frac{d\cdot e}{2d^{2}e^{2}}$). So

$$\int^{R}\!\!d^{2}r\;\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Big[-\ell_{k}+\log|r|\Big] \;\sim\;-c\,\ell_{k}\log R+\frac{c}{2}\log^{2}R,\qquad c=2\pi\big\langle T_{2}\big\rangle\rho^{2}.$$

The truncated integral confirms it, and quantitatively:

$R$$F(R)$$dF/d\log R$change per decade
$10^{2}$$-6.48980$——
$10^{3}$$-10.05680$$-1.5491$—
$10^{4}$$-12.68358$$-1.1408$$+0.4083$
$10^{5}$$-14.37029$$-0.7325$$+0.4083$
$10^{6}$$-15.12141$$-0.3262$$+0.4063$

The slope does not settle — it grows linearly in $\log R$, by a constant $0.4083$ per decade. That is the signature of $\frac{c}{2}\log^{2}R$, and the rate is predicted exactly:

$$c=2\pi\times0.028221=0.17732,\qquad c\log 10=0.4083. $$

Both pieces are divergent: $-c\,\ell_{k}\log R$ is the transverse divergence multiplying the rapidity log, and $\frac{c}{2}\log^{2}R$ is the transverse divergence squared. The second is precisely where the $\ell_{k}^{2}$ comes from once $R$ is cut by $1/(\xi|k|)$.

4. Summary, and one Mathematica caution

Why is $\mathrm{Ei}$ undefined at $\Lambda\to0$?rapidity divergence, $\log\lambda=-\ell_{k}$ — real
Why also at $y'\to z$?$\mathrm{Ei}$ sees only $\kappa\lambda$; it is the same log
Does $y'\to z$ mean a transverse divergence?No — a log integrates fine in 2D; $F\propto\epsilon^{2}$
Can you do the $+$ prescription?Yes, as an operation; it is an exact identity
Are the two resulting terms separately finite?No — large $|y'-z|$, §3

Branch caution. ExpIntegralEi of an imaginary argument sits on a branch cut. The relation to the principal–branch $E_{1}$ is

$$\mathrm{Ei}(-iu)=-E_{1}(iu)-i\pi\,{\rm sgn}(u)\qquad(u\ \text{real}),$$

checked to $10^{-10}$. In your difference the $-i\pi\,{\rm sgn}$ cancels, since $\Xi$ and $\lambda$ are both positive — so the bracket is safe. But do not drop $\mathrm{Ei}(-i\kappa\Xi)$ when taking $\vee\to\infty$: it tends to the constant $-i\pi\,{\rm sgn}\,\kappa$, not to zero, and that constant is a genuine absorptive contribution.
