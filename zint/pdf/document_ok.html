The $1/\bar\epsilon$ in Row IV: what it is, and where it goes

Answering your advisor's five statements, one at a time

$\xi=\dfrac{k^{+}}{k^{+}\!+p^{+}}$,  $\bar\xi=1-\xi$,  $\xi_{0}=\dfrac{k^{+}}{\vee}<1$,  $r=y'-y$,  $L_{r}=\log\big(4\pi^{2}\mu^{2}r^{2}\big)$,  $\dfrac{1}{\bar\epsilon}=\dfrac1\epsilon-\gamma_{E}+\log4\pi$,  $\widehat{\mathcal C}(\xi)=\xi\bar\xi+\dfrac{\xi}{[1-\xi]_{+}}+\dfrac{\bar\xi}{\xi}=\dfrac{\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}$

Short answer. Your advisor is right on all five points of principle, and their expression is algebraically identical to yours — the apparent sign difference is entirely in the reversed integration limits $\int_{1}^{\xi_{0}}=-\int_{\xi_{0}}^{1}$. But the prescription in their line 2 cannot be right as it stands, for a structural reason: this row contains exactly one $1/\bar\epsilon$, and that single pole multiplies $\big[\delta(1-\xi)\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}+\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}\big]$ as a whole. It therefore has one fate, not two. An $\overline{\rm MS}$ fragmentation counterterm is proportional to $\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)$ alone — it has no $\delta(1-\xi)\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ — so it can remove only part of the pole and must leave the rest behind, which is exactly the leftover they are objecting to. And the pole is a large–$|z|$ (infrared, large transverse distance) divergence, not a collinear one: the $z\!\to\!y$ region is convergent. My reading is that it cancels against the companion Group II rows through the BK identity, and that nothing should be absorbed into a fragmentation function here.

1. The advisor's expression versus yours: they are the same

Write the row's non-$\delta$ integrand with the common prefactor $\mathcal P_{N}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{g^{4}}{8\pi^{4}}\frac{N_{c}}{k^{+}}$ stripped, and abbreviate

$$\mathcal X=\frac{(x-y)\!\cdot\!(x'-y')}{(x-y)^{2}(x'-y')^{2}},\qquad \mathcal R=\frac{(x-y)\!\cdot\!r\;(x'-y')\!\cdot\!r}{(x-y)^{2}(x'-y')^{2}r^{2}},\qquad \mathcal M=\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}+1-L_{r}.$$

Yours, with the measure written in the natural order $\int_{\xi_{0}}^{1}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}$:

$$\mathcal D=\widehat{\mathcal C}\,\mathcal M\,\mathcal X\;-\;d_{\perp}\,\xi\bar\xi\,\mathcal R \;-\;\Big[\tfrac{\xi}{[1-\xi]_{+}}+\tfrac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big]\mathcal X .$$

Theirs, reading each of their lines 2, 3, 4 exactly as written, with $\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}}{2}=N_{c}\widehat{\mathcal C}$ and their $\frac{1}{k^{+}}$ in line 4 supplying the missing $N_{c}$:

$$\mathcal A=\underbrace{-\,\widehat{\mathcal C}\,\tfrac{1}{\bar\epsilon}\,\mathcal X}_{\text{their line 2}} \;\underbrace{+\;d_{\perp}\xi\bar\xi\,\mathcal R+\Big[\tfrac{\xi}{[1-\xi]_{+}}+\tfrac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big]\mathcal X}_{\text{their line 3}} \;\underbrace{-\;\widehat{\mathcal C}\,(1-L_{r})\,\mathcal X}_{\text{their line 4}} .$$

Sympy gives $\mathcal A+\mathcal D=0$ identically. So $\mathcal A=-\mathcal D$. But every one of their $\xi$-integrals is written $\int_{1}^{k^{+}/\vee}$, and since $\xi_{0}=k^{+}/\vee<1$ this is a reversed integration, $\int_{1}^{\xi_{0}}=-\int_{\xi_{0}}^{1}$. The two minus signs cancel:

$$\int_{1}^{\xi_{0}}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}\;\mathcal A \;=\;\int_{\xi_{0}}^{1}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}\;\mathcal D\qquad\text{exactly.}$$

This is worth being explicit about with your advisor, because it is a trap: if anyone later reads $\int_{1}^{k^{+}/\vee}$ as if it were positively ordered, the whole non-$\delta$ sector of the cross section flips sign relative to the evolution term. I would write all four lines with $\int_{\xi_{0}}^{1}$ and put the signs on the terms.

Two further points of agreement, which are reassuring:

  • Their line 1 (evolution) carries $\big[\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-L_{r}\big]$ with no $+1$, while their line 4 carries $\big[1-L_{r}\big]$ with the $+1$. That asymmetry is correct and not a typo: the $\frac{1}{p^{+}}$ block of the row comes as $\frac{1}{p^{+}}\mathcal M\mathcal X-\frac{1}{p^{+}}\mathcal X =\frac{1}{p^{+}}\big(\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-L_{r}\big)\mathcal X$, so the $+1$ has already cancelled on whatever the $\frac{1}{p^{+}}$ pole produces — and the $\delta(1-\xi)$ of the plus prescription is produced by exactly that pole.
  • Their $\log\frac{\vee}{\Lambda}+\log\frac{k^{+}}{\vee}=\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ is the rapidity factorisation split: the first piece is absorbed into the JIMWLK evolution of the target from $\Lambda$ up to $\vee$, the second is the finite NLO remainder. That is the right thing to do and it agrees with your $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$.
One normalisation to check. Their line 2 is written as $-\frac{g^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}\int\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}}{2} \frac{d^{3}N_{\rm LO}}{d^{3}k}\big(\frac{k}{\xi}\big)$. For that to match the prefactor $\mathcal P_{N}$ carried by lines 1, 3, 4, you need $\frac{d^{3}N_{\rm LO}}{d^{3}k}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{g^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{+}} \int_{x,x',y,y'}\mathcal X\,\mathcal U$. Compare this against your LO row before you accept their line 2; if the LO normalisation is different, line 2 is inconsistent with lines 1, 3, 4 by that ratio.

2. It simplifies further, and the simplification is the whole point

Neither version is fully reduced. The $+1$ inside $\mathcal M$ cancels against the explicit $-\big[\frac{\xi}{[1-\xi]_{+}}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\big]\mathcal X$, because those are two of the three terms of $\widehat{\mathcal C}$. Term by term, with $\widehat{\mathcal C}=\xi\bar\xi+\frac{\xi}{[1-\xi]_{+}}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}$,

$$\underbrace{\Big[\tfrac{\xi}{[1-\xi]_{+}}+\tfrac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big]\big(\mathcal M-1\big)\mathcal X}_{+1\ \text{gone}} \;+\;\underbrace{\xi\bar\xi\,\mathcal M\,\mathcal X-d_{\perp}\xi\bar\xi\,\mathcal R}_{+1\ \text{survives here}} \;=\;\widehat{\mathcal C}\Big[\tfrac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-L_{r}\Big]\mathcal X+\xi\bar\xi\big[\mathcal X-d_{\perp}\mathcal R\big].$$

(Sympy: difference $=0$.) Adding the $\delta$ sector, which carries $\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-L_{r}$ as noted above, the entire row collapses to

$$\frac{d^{3}N^{\rm NLO}}{d^{3}k}\Big|_{\rm Row\,IV}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{g^{4}}{8\pi^{4}}\frac{N_{c}}{k^{+}} \int_{\xi_{0}}^{1}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}\;e^{-i\frac{k}{\xi}\cdot r}\int_{x,x',y,y'}\Bigg\{$$ $$\Big[\delta(1-\xi)\log\tfrac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}+\tfrac{\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}\Big] \Big[\tfrac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-\log\big(4\pi^{2}\mu^{2}r^{2}\big)\Big]\mathcal X \;+\;\xi(1-\xi)\big[\mathcal X-d_{\perp}\mathcal R\big]\Bigg\}\,\mathcal U$$

Four lines have become two, and now the structure your advisor is worried about is visible in one glance: there is a single $\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}$, and it does not belong to the $\mathcal P_{gg}$ term alone. Its coefficient is $\delta(1-\xi)\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}+\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}$. The last block, $\xi(1-\xi)\big[\mathcal X-d_{\perp}\mathcal R\big]$, is finite, $\mu$-independent and $\Lambda$-independent — it is the genuine NLO hard remainder.

4. Is this pole collinear? No — it is a large–distance pole

This decides everything, so do it by hand. The pole comes from

$$\mathcal G^{mm'}=\int d^{2}z\;\frac{(y-z)^{m}\,(y'-z)^{m'}}{(y-z)^{2}\,(y'-z)^{2}},$$ which has two potentially dangerous regions.

(a) Short distance, $z\to y$ (the emitted gluon collinear to the measured one)

Put $u=y-z$, $|u|=\rho\to0$, and use $y'-z=u+r\to r$:

$$\frac{u^{m}\,(u+r)^{m'}}{u^{2}\,(u+r)^{2}}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{u^{m}}{\rho^{2}}\cdot\frac{r^{m'}}{r^{2}} \;\sim\;\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot\frac{1}{|r|},\qquad d^{2}z=\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi,$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad \int_{0}\rho\,d\rho\;\frac{1}{\rho}\;\frac{1}{|r|}=\int_{0}\frac{d\rho}{|r|}\ \ \textbf{finite.}$$

The single power of $u^{m}$ in the numerator softens the $1/u^{2}$ to $1/\rho$, and the two-dimensional measure $\rho\,d\rho$ then wins. There is no collinear divergence in this integral. The same holds at $z\to y'$ by symmetry.

(b) Large distance, $|z|\to\infty$

$$\frac{(y-z)^{m}(y'-z)^{m'}}{(y-z)^{2}(y'-z)^{2}}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{z^{m}z^{m'}}{z^{4}}\sim\frac{1}{z^{2}}, \qquad d^{2}z=|z|\,d|z|\,d\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int^{R}\frac{d|z|}{|z|}=\log R\ \ \textbf{divergent.}$$

With a hard cutoff $|z|<R$ this is the result you already have,

$$\mathcal G^{mm'}\Big|_{\rm cutoff}=\frac{\pi}{2}\Big(\log\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}+1\Big)\delta^{mm'}-\pi\,\hat r^{m}\hat r^{m'},$$

in which $R$ is the infrared cutoff and $r$ only sets the scale. Dimensional regularisation trades $\log R^{2}$ for $\frac{1}{\epsilon}$, so

The $\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}$ of Row IV is infrared — it comes from the emitted gluon sitting arbitrarily far away in transverse position (arbitrarily small transverse momentum). It is not a collinear pole. A fragmentation function absorbs collinear poles. It has no business absorbing this one.

5. What it really cancels against: the BK identity

In the CGC the large–$|z|$ region is not divergent in the full answer, because the emitted gluon carries a Wilson line $U(z)$ and $U(z)\to\mathbb 1$ as $|z|\to\infty$; the real emission is then cancelled by the virtual ones. Algebraically that cancellation is the identity

$$\frac{(y-z)\!\cdot\!(y'-z)}{(y-z)^{2}(y'-z)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2(y-z)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2(y'-z)^{2}} =-\,\frac{(y-y')^{2}}{2\,(y-z)^{2}(y'-z)^{2}}.$$

(Sympy: difference $=0$.) The right-hand side falls as $1/|z|^{4}$, so $\int d^{2}z$ of it converges: the log at large $|z|$ is gone. The first term is Row IV; the two subtractions are the virtual/self-energy partners in Group II. Numerically, with $|r|=1$ and a cutoff at $|z|=R$:

The real term alone climbs by $\pi\log 9=6.9$ per column, exactly $\pi\log\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}$; the combination saturates. That is the pole disappearing.

There is a second, independent argument that points the same way, and it is the one I would put to your advisor. The coefficient of $\delta(1-\xi)\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ in the boxed result of §2 is, by construction, $\big(\text{LO}\big)\times\big(\text{the JIMWLK kernel}\big)$: that is what a rapidity-evolution term is. The JIMWLK kernel is finite. Therefore the bracket $\big[\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-\log(4\pi^{2}\mu^{2}r^{2})\big]$ must become finite once the companion colour structures are restored. And because the very same bracket multiplies $\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}$, it becomes finite there at the same moment, for free.

6. Why the half-and-half prescription is not self-consistent

Suppose one insisted on their line 2 — absorb $\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}}{2\bar\epsilon}$ into a gluon fragmentation function. Then:

  1. The $\delta(1-\xi)\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}$ piece is untouched, because no $\overline{\rm MS}$ fragmentation counterterm contains a rapidity logarithm $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$. It would still have to cancel against the companion rows.
  2. But it is the same $\int d^{2}z$. If that $z$-integral's divergence cancels against the companion rows for the $\delta(1-\xi)$ piece, it cancels for every $\xi$ — the $z$-integral does not know about $\xi$ at all.
  3. So the counterterm in line 2 would be subtracting a pole that is already cancelling: a double count, leaving a spurious finite $-\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}}{2}\times(\text{scheme})$ behind.

The logic closes: the $\xi$-independence of the $z$-integral is what forbids the split.

7. What I would say back

  • Agree with the principle. A $\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}$ in a final answer is not acceptable, and $\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}}{\bar\epsilon}$ is a counterterm, not DGLAP. Both of those are right.
  • Show the compact form of §2. It makes the single-pole structure manifest and it is four lines shorter than either version. Their line 3 and the $+1$ in their line 4 cancel each other.
  • State where the pole lives. $\int d^{2}z$ at large $|z|$, and give the two-line power counting of §4(a)–(b) showing the $z\to y$ region is convergent. This is the fact that settles “absorb or cancel”, and it is checkable in thirty seconds.
  • Propose the check instead of asserting the answer. The honest position is that Row IV alone cannot decide its own fate: the companion Group II contractions (the ones that keep $U(z)$ inside the $z$-integral, and the virtual/self-energy rows) have not been computed yet. Compute two of them and test the BK identity of §5 on the sum. If the $\log R^{2}$ cancels, the pole was never collinear and no fragmentation function is needed at this order in this row.
  • Where $\log\mu_{F}$ will come from. After the cancellation, $\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)$ multiplies $\frac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}z\;\frac{-(y-y')^{2}}{2(y-z)^{2}(y'-z)^{2}}$ — finite, $\mu$-independent, a logarithm of a ratio of dipole sizes rather than of $\mu_{F}$. That is the CGC form of the collinear logarithm: $\log\frac{1}{r^{2}\mu_{F}^{2}}$ only appears once you additionally factorise the small-$r$ region into a fragmentation function at scale $\mu_{F}$, which is a separate step, done after Group II is summed and not before.
What I cannot prove from here. That the cancellation actually goes through. Row IV is one colour contraction; the statement “the pole cancels” is a statement about the sum over Group II, and the other members are not yet in hand. Everything above — the identity of the two expressions, the collapse to one pole, the infrared (not collinear) nature of that pole, the BK identity and its numerics — is checked. The fate is an argued expectation, not a checked result. Say so; it is a stronger position than either “it cancels” or “absorb it” asserted flat.
