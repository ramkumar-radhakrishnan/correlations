The $\mathfrak A^{(1)\dagger}\bar{\mathfrak C}^{\dagger}\times\mathfrak B_{2}$ interference

Full check, the divergence structure, and why the $+$ prescription fails here

$S=p^{+}\!+k^{+}$,  $A=(x-z)^{2}$,  $B=(x-w)^{2}$,  $C=(z-w)^{2}$,  $D=p^{+}A+k^{+}B$,  $\mathcal V_{w}=\frac{z-w}{C}-\frac{x-w}{2B}$,  $\mathcal V_{z}=\frac{z-w}{C}+\frac{x-z}{2A}$

The calculation is right except for one typo (§3), and there is a subtlety in the prefactor that is also right but must be stated or it looks wrong (§1). The $w'$ integration, the phase split, and all six longitudinal weights are exact (§4). On divergences: no UV, and — unlike the two–$\mathfrak C$ row — no collinear pole either, because here the $z$ integral carries the phase $e^{+i\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}k\cdot z}$, which cuts the would–be collinear logarithm at $|z|\sim k^{+}/(p^{+}|k_{\perp}|)$ (§8). So there is nothing to put in $\overline{\rm MS}$ and no fragmentation subtraction to make here. But that cutoff grows as $p^{+}\to0$, and the result is a double logarithm: $$I(p^{+})\,p^{+}=0.1085-0.0490\,\log\frac{k^{+}}{p^{+}}\qquad\text{(stable under a factor 4 in cutoff and grid)}$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad \int_{\Lambda}\frac{dp^{+}}{p^{+}}\log\frac{k^{+}}{p^{+}}\;\sim\;\tfrac12\log^{2}\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}.$$ Therefore you cannot use the same $+$ prescription here (§10): it needs a finite endpoint value $g(0)$, and here $g$ diverges logarithmically at the endpoint.

1. The prefactor — and the factor hidden in your rewritten $\delta^{(2)}$

Take the three coefficients, all evaluated at the arguments the row uses ($\mathfrak A^{(1)}$ and $\mathfrak C_{1}$ at $k^{+}_{\rm arg}=S$):

$$\mathfrak A^{(1)\dagger}=-\frac{ig}{\sqrt2\,\pi\sqrt S},\qquad \bar{\mathfrak C}^{\dagger}=-\frac{g}{2\pi\sqrt{2S}}\frac{\sqrt{p^{+}k^{+}}}{S},\qquad \mathfrak B_{2}=\frac{ig^{2}\sqrt{p^{+}k^{+}}}{4\pi^{2}\,D\,S}$$

(the last uses $\sqrt{p^{+}(S-p^{+})}=\sqrt{p^{+}k^{+}}$). Multiplying and attaching $\frac{4}{(2\pi)^{3}}$:

$$\frac{4}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\mathfrak A^{\dagger}\bar{\mathfrak C}^{\dagger}\mathfrak B_{2} =-\frac{g^{4}}{32\pi^{7}}\,\frac{p^{+}k^{+}}{S^{3}D} =-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{g^{4}}{4\pi^{4}}\,\frac{p^{+}k^{+}}{S^{3}D}$$

while you wrote $\;-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{g^{4}}{4\pi^{4}}\frac{1}{k^{+}}\frac{p^{+}}{S\,D}$. The ratio is

$$\frac{\text{yours}}{\text{derived}}=\frac{S^{2}}{k^{+2}}\;\neq\;1\ \text{---}\ \text{but this is not an error.}$$
Where it goes. The definition's delta is $\delta^{(2)}\big(y'-w'+\frac{p^{+}}{S}(w'-z)\big)$. You wrote $\delta^{(2)}\big(\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}(y'-z)+y'-w'\big)$. These arguments are proportional: $$\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}(y'-z)+y'-w'=\frac{S}{k^{+}}\left[y'-w'+\frac{p^{+}}{S}(w'-z)\right]$$ (check: multiply out the right side, $\frac{S}{k^{+}}y'-\frac{S}{k^{+}}w'+\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}w'-\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}z =y'+\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}y'-w'-\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}z$ ✓). Since $\delta^{(2)}(\lambda v)=\lambda^{-2}\delta^{(2)}(v)$ in two dimensions, your delta carries an extra $k^{+2}/S^{2}$ — which is exactly cancelled by the $S^{2}/k^{+2}$ in your prefactor. Net ratio $=1$, verified symbolically. Worth a footnote in your draft, or a reader will stop here.

2. The overall minus sign

$\mathfrak A^{\dagger}$ carries $-i$, $\bar{\mathfrak C}^{\dagger}$ carries $-1$ (it is real), $\mathfrak B_{2}$ carries $+i$. Product: $(-i)(-1)(+i)=i\cdot i=-1$. Your explicit minus is right, and the row is real, as an interference of this type must be once it is added to its conjugate.

3. The tensor contraction — and the one typo

3.1 Setting it up

Write the $\mathfrak B_{2}$ bracket as $W^{ji}$ with the factorised form established earlier:

$$W^{ji}=\delta^{ji}\frac{A-B}{2C}+\frac{S}{k^{+}}(x-z)^{j}\,\mathcal V_{w}^{\,i} +\frac{S}{p^{+}}\,\mathcal V_{z}^{\,j}\,(x-w)^{i}$$

The $\mathfrak C$ bracket is called as $\bar{\mathfrak C}_{1\,j\,i\,k'}$, i.e. with the second and third index slots holding $i$ and $k'$, whereas the definition $\mathfrak C_{1\,j\,k\,i}$ holds $k$ and $i$. So relabel the definition's $k\to i$ and $i\to k'$:

$$\big[\delta_{im}\delta_{jk}-\tfrac{S}{p^{+}}\delta_{jm}\delta_{ik}-\tfrac{S}{k^{+}}\delta_{km}\delta_{ij}\big] \;\longrightarrow\; \big[\delta_{k'm}\delta_{ij}-\tfrac{S}{p^{+}}\delta_{jm}\delta_{ik'}-\tfrac{S}{k^{+}}\delta_{im}\delta_{jk'}\big]$$

3.2 Contracting over $i,j$

Take the three deltas one at a time, exactly as in the previous note:

  • $\delta_{k'm}\delta_{ij}W^{ji}=\delta_{k'm}\,W^{ii}=\delta_{k'm}\operatorname{tr}W$;
  • $-\frac{S}{p^{+}}\delta_{jm}\delta_{ik'}W^{ji}=-\frac{S}{p^{+}}W^{mk'}$ (set $j=m$, $i=k'$);

$$R^{mk'}=\delta_{k'm}\,\operatorname{tr}W\;-\;\frac{S}{p^{+}}\,W^{mk'}\;-\;\frac{S}{k^{+}}\,W^{k'm}$$

Note $W$ is not symmetric (unlike $T^{mm'}$), so the last two do not merge.

10. Your three questions, answered

10.1 Is there a UV divergence to put in $\overline{\rm MS}$?

No. All 26 short–distance regions are convergent (§7). There is no $\frac{1}{\epsilon}$ in this row, so dimensional regularisation has nothing to do and $\overline{\rm MS}$ has nothing to subtract.

10.2 Is there a collinear divergence to absorb into a fragmentation function?

No — not at fixed $p^{+}$. There is a collinear region (large $|z|$), and it is the same region that gave the fragmentation pole in the two–$\mathfrak C$ row. But there the $z$ integral was phase–free (both $\mathfrak C$'s deltas had been used, leaving the phase in $y'-y$ only), so it produced a genuine $\int^{\infty}\frac{d^{2}z}{z^{2}}$ and a $\frac1\epsilon$. Here only one $\mathfrak C$ delta exists, the other side is $\mathfrak B_{2}$, and the leftover phase $e^{+i\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}k\cdot z}$ sits directly on $z$ and cuts the integral off. The result is a finite logarithm of a physical ratio, not a pole. Nothing to subtract into $D_{g/g}$ from this row.

10.3 Can you use the same $+$ prescription for the $p^{+}$ integration?

No. The $+$ prescription is defined by $$\int_{0}^{P}\!dp^{+}\left[\frac{1}{p^{+}}\right]_{+}g(p^{+})=\int_{0}^{P}\!dp^{+}\,\frac{g(p^{+})-g(0)}{p^{+}}$$ and it is meaningful only if $g(0)$ exists. Here $g(p^{+})=p^{+}I(p^{+})$ and §9 shows $g(p^{+})=a+b\log\frac{k^{+}}{p^{+}}$, which diverges logarithmically as $p^{+}\to0$. There is no $g(0)$ to subtract, so the naive prescription is ill–defined: it would leave you subtracting infinity from infinity.

Contrast the two–$\mathfrak C$ row, where I told you the $+$ prescription is legitimate: there, after the $z$ integral, the only $\zeta$–dependence left in $g$ was the smooth phase $e^{-ik\cdot r/\zeta}$, analytic at the endpoint. The difference is not the prescription — it is whether anything singular survives in $g$ at the subtraction point.

What you can do instead. Two routes, both standard:

  1. Two–step subtraction. Split $g(p^{+})=b\log\frac{k^{+}}{p^{+}}+\tilde g(p^{+})$ with $\tilde g$ now regular at the endpoint. The second piece takes an ordinary $\big[\frac{1}{p^{+}}\big]_{+}$; the first integrates to the explicit $\frac b2\log^{2}\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$, which you keep as a $\log^{2}$ and match against the partner rows.
  2. Keep $\Lambda$. Do not prescribe anything; carry $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ and $\log^{2}\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ explicitly. A $\log^{2}$ at this order is the signature of an $\mathcal O(\alpha_{s})$ correction to the evolution kernel, so it has to be matched, not prescribed away.
One honest caveat. The $\log^{2}$ is established for this interference. It is one of a conjugate pair (its partner $\mathfrak B_{2}^{\dagger}\times\mathfrak A\bar{\mathfrak C}$ is the complex conjugate, so the pair gives $2\,\mathrm{Re}$ and the double log doubles rather than cancels). Whether it survives in the full NLO sum depends on the remaining rows — that cannot be decided from this row alone, and I have not checked it.
