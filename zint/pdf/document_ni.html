Doing the $+$ prescription first

Can the $p^{+}$ integral be done with no transverse integration at all? — yes, and here is why

$S\equiv p^{+}+k^{+}$,  $r\equiv y'-y$,  $\kappa\equiv\dfrac{k\cdot r}{k^{+}}$,  $P_{\vee}\equiv\vee-k^{+}$

Yes. In this row $p^{+}$ touches the transverse coordinates in exactly one place — the phase $e^{-i\kappa p^{+}}$, which involves $y$ and $y'$ and nothing else. Every kernel, the whole $z$ integral, and the entire colour structure are $p^{+}$–independent, so they are a spectator factor that pulls straight out of the $p^{+}$ integral. The $+$ prescription therefore acts on a pure phase. Verified: the two integrations commute to the accuracy of the quadrature (§2), and the $p^{+}\to0$ subtraction term is still transverse–convergent (§3). Two things to keep in mind: the $\frac{2}{k^{+}}$ term is only zero under $\int d^{2}z$, so you must carry it (§4); and the $z$ integral is still infrared divergent, so $\epsilon$ is still required eventually — the $+$ prescription does not remove that (§5).

1. The form you can write down

Keeping every transverse coordinate un-integrated:

$$\frac{d^{3}N^{\rm NLO}}{d^{3}k}\bigg|_{\mathfrak C\mathfrak C} =\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\frac{g^{4}}{4\pi^{4}}\,\frac{1}{k^{+}} \int_{0}^{\vee-k^{+}}\frac{dp^{+}}{2\pi}\int_{x,x',y,y',z} e^{-ik\cdot(y'-y)}\;e^{-i\kappa p^{+}}\; \mathcal W^{mm'kk'}\;\mathcal B_{mm'kk'}(p^{+})\;\mathcal C$$

with the purely transverse factor, the purely longitudinal bracket, and the colour

$$\mathcal W^{mm'kk'}=\frac{(y-z)^{m}\,(y'-z)^{m'}\,(x-y)^{k}\,(x'-y')^{k'}} {(y-z)^{2}\,(y'-z)^{2}\,(x-y)^{2}\,(x'-y')^{2}},$$ $$\mathcal B_{mm'kk'}(p^{+})=\frac{d_{\perp}p^{+}}{(k^{+}+p^{+})^{2}}\delta_{km}\delta_{k'm'} +\frac{2}{k^{+}}\big(\delta_{mk'}\delta_{m'k}-\delta_{m'k'}\delta_{km}\big) +\frac{p^{+}}{k^{+2}}\delta_{mm'}\delta_{kk'} +\frac{1}{p^{+}}\delta_{mm'}\delta_{kk'},$$ $$\mathcal C=N_{c}\big[U^{e'c}(y')-U^{e'c}(x')\big]\big[U^{ec}(y)-U^{ec}(x)\big]\rho^{e'}(x')\rho^{e}(x),$$

and the single replacement, understood under $\int_{0}^{\vee-k^{+}}dp^{+}$:

$$\frac{1}{p^{+}}\;\longrightarrow\;\left[\frac{1}{p^{+}}\right]_{+}+\;\delta(p^{+})\,\log\frac{\vee-k^{+}}{\Lambda}, \qquad \int_{0}^{P_{\vee}}\!\!dp^{+}\left[\frac{1}{p^{+}}\right]_{+}\!h(p^{+}) \equiv\int_{0}^{P_{\vee}}\frac{dp^{+}}{p^{+}}\Big[h(p^{+})-h(0)\Big].$$

Because $\mathcal W$ and $\mathcal C$ carry no $p^{+}$, the function the prescription acts on is just $h(p^{+})=e^{-i\kappa p^{+}}$, with $h(0)=1$. Explicitly, for the $\frac{1}{p^{+}}$ term:

$$\int_{\Lambda}^{\vee-k^{+}}\frac{dp^{+}}{p^{+}}\,e^{-i\kappa p^{+}}\;\mathcal W^{mm'kk'}\delta_{mm'}\delta_{kk'}\,\mathcal C =\left\{\log\frac{\vee-k^{+}}{\Lambda}+\int_{0}^{\vee-k^{+}}\frac{dp^{+}}{p^{+}}\Big[e^{-i\kappa p^{+}}-1\Big]\right\} \mathcal W^{mm'kk'}\delta_{mm'}\delta_{kk'}\,\mathcal C .$$

Checked numerically against the direct integral at fixed transverse points: agreement to $1.2\times10^{-9}$ with $\Lambda=10^{-9}$, i.e. the identity is exact up to the expected $O(\kappa\Lambda)$ remainder. The other three terms of $\mathcal B$ are regular at $p^{+}=0$ and need no prescription — just integrate them from $0$.

3. Why it is safe here, when it was not before

The obstruction in the earlier rows was never the ultraviolet. It was this: the $p^{+}\to0$ subtraction replaces $h(p^{+})$ by $h(0)$, and if $p^{+}$ sits inside a transverse phase then setting $p^{+}=0$ strips that oscillation, leaving a bare $1/z^{2}$ tail that the phase had been damping. Here that cannot happen:

$$w-z=\frac{S}{k^{+}}(y-z)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad w'-w=\frac{S}{k^{+}}(y'-y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-ik\cdot(w'-w)}=\underbrace{e^{-ik\cdot(y'-y)}}_{\text{no }p^{+}}\; \underbrace{e^{-i\kappa p^{+}}}_{\text{no }z,\,x,\,x'} .$$

The two $\delta^{(2)}$'s moved every trace of $z$ out of the phase. So:

  • $z$, $x$, $x'$ never see $p^{+}$ at all. Nothing about them changes under the subtraction.
  • $y$, $y'$ see $p^{+}$ only through $e^{-i\kappa p^{+}}$; at $p^{+}=0$ the phase becomes $e^{-ik\cdot(y'-y)}$, not $1$. The leading–order oscillation survives, so the Bessel $R^{-1/2}$ damping that makes the large–$|y|$ region converge is still there. Measured on the subtraction term itself:

4. What leaving $z$ un-integrated costs you

The $\frac{2}{k^{+}}\big(\delta_{mk'}\delta_{m'k}-\delta_{m'k'}\delta_{km}\big)$ term cancels only after $\int d^{2}z$, because that is when the tensor $T^{mm'}=\int d^{2}z\,\frac{(y-z)^{m}(y'-z)^{m'}}{(y-z)^{2}(y'-z)^{2}}$ becomes symmetric. Pointwise in $z$ it is

$$\frac{2}{k^{+}}\Big[\big(M\!\cdot\!X'\big)\big(N\!\cdot\!X\big)-\big(M\!\cdot\!X\big)\big(N\!\cdot\!X'\big)\Big], \qquad M=\tfrac{y-z}{(y-z)^{2}},\;N=\tfrac{y'-z}{(y'-z)^{2}},$$

which is not zero: measured maximum $173.7$ on the grid, integrating to $1.5\times10^{-3}$ (i.e. zero, at quadrature accuracy). So carry it. It is harmless, but do not drop it or make any approximation to the $z$ dependence before integrating, or you will keep a spurious remainder.

5. What the $+$ prescription does not do for you

It does not regulate $z$. The trace of the $z$ integral is still $\pi\log\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}$ — at $R=300$ in the test above, $33.24$ — and that logarithm diverges as $R\to\infty$ at every value of $p^{+}$, including $p^{+}=0$. You still need $d=2-2\epsilon$ (or a cutoff) on the $z$ integral.

The good news is that §2 says the two regulators commute, so you are free to:

  1. apply $\big[\tfrac{1}{p^{+}}\big]_{+}$ now, keeping all five transverse integrals intact,
  2. extract $\log\frac{\vee-k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ and hand it to JIMWLK — it multiplies the $p^{+}\to0$ limit of the transverse structure, which is the LO kernel times the real BK kernel,
  3. regulate and do the $z$ integral whenever it suits you.

The answer is the same either way.
