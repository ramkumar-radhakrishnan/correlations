Group I, Row V, part (b): the UV log in transverse coordinates

Replacing $\log\frac{\mu^{2}}{k^{2}}$ by $\log\big(\mu^{2}(y-x)^{2}\big)$: the coordinate-space counterterm, done in $d=2-2\epsilon$, then the $p^{+}$ integral — every step, each one checked numerically

$r=z-x$,  $s=y-x$,  $P=x'-w'$,  $\xi=\dfrac{p^{+}}{k^{+}}$,  $\bar\xi=1-\xi$,  $q=\bar\xi k$,  $D=(r-s)^{2}+\dfrac{\xi}{\bar\xi}s^{2}$,  $\lambda=\dfrac{\Lambda}{k^{+}}$,  $L=\log\dfrac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$,  $\mathcal N=\dfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\dfrac{g^{4}}{16\pi^{4}}\dfrac{1}{k^{+}}$,  $C_{\rm UV}(\xi)=\xi\bar\xi+\dfrac{\xi}{\bar\xi}+\dfrac{\bar\xi}{\xi}$,  $\mathcal W=\dfrac{P\cdot s}{P^{2}s^{2}}$,  $\mathcal T=\dfrac{P\cdot s-2(P\cdot\hat k)(s\cdot\hat k)}{P^{2}s^{2}}$

The answer. $$\frac{d^{3}N_{b}}{d^{3}k}=\mathcal N\!\!\int_{x,y,x',w'}\!\!e^{-ik\cdot(w'-x)}\bigg\{\mathcal W\Big(2L-\frac{11}{6}\Big)\Big[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}\,(y-x)^{2}\,e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}\Big] +\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal W\,L^{2}+\frac{\tilde\beta_{0}+\tilde\beta_{1}}{\pi}\,L+\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal F}_{b}}{\pi}\bigg\}\,\mathcal U_{b}$$
  • The pole and its coefficient $2L-\frac{11}{6}$ are the same as before. Only the scale in the log changes, from $\frac{1}{k^{2}}$ to $\frac{(y-x)^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}$.
  • The log vanishes at $\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}=\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{(y-x)^{2}}$ with $c_{0}=2e^{-\gamma_{E}}$, the usual coordinate-space scale.
  • $\tilde\beta_{0,1}$ and $\widetilde{\mathcal F}_{b}$ are finite and free of $\epsilon$ and $\Lambda$ (§7). The total is identical to the momentum-space result of rowV_full_derivation.pdf (§8); only the split between the UV term and the remainder differs.

0. Where we start, and why the log came out in $k$

After the $w$ integration, part (b) of the .tex is (see rowV_full_derivation.pdf, §§0–2)

$$\frac{d^{3}N_{b}}{d^{3}k}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{g^{4}}{16\pi^{5}}\frac{1}{k^{+}}\int_{x,y,x',w'}e^{-ik\cdot(w'-x)}\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!d\xi\int d^{2}r\;e^{iq\cdot r}\,\frac{T(r)}{\bar\xi D}\;\mathcal U_{b}, \qquad \mathcal U_{b}\ \text{independent of }z.$$

As $r\to0$ we have $D\to s^{2}+\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}s^{2}=\frac{s^{2}}{\bar\xi}$, so $\bar\xi D\to s^{2}$ and $e^{iq\cdot r}\to1$. Of the six blocks, only the pieces going like $\frac{1}{r^{2}}$ survive:

$$\frac{T(r)}{\bar\xi D}\ \xrightarrow{r\to0}\ \frac{T_{\rm UV}(r)}{s^{2}},\qquad T_{\rm UV}(r)=d_{\perp}\,\xi\bar\xi\,\frac{(P\cdot r)(s\cdot r)}{P^{2}r^{4}}+\Big(\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big)\frac{P\cdot s}{P^{2}r^{2}}.$$

(The UV pieces of $T_{3},T_{5},T_{6}$ cancel pointwise.) Since $\int d^{2}r\,\frac{1}{r^{2}}$ diverges logarithmically at small $r$, the divergence is a log. A log needs a second scale to make its argument dimensionless, and that scale is set by how the subtraction term is cut off at large $r$:

  • In the earlier PDF I subtracted $e^{iq\cdot r}\frac{T_{\rm UV}}{s^{2}}$. Its large-$r$ cut-off is the oscillation at $|r|\sim\frac{1}{q}$, so the log came out as $\log\frac{\mu^{2}}{q^{2}}=\log\frac{\mu^{2}}{\bar\xi^{2}k^{2}}$.
  • To get $\log\mu^{2}(y-x)^{2}$ instead, subtract a term without the phase, cut off at $|r|\sim|s|=|y-x|$.

Both choices are legitimate. Each is just a way of writing $0$ as $(-S+S)$; the physics is in the sum.

1. The coordinate-space counterterm

$$S(r)\equiv\frac{T_{\rm UV}(r)}{r^{2}+s^{2}}=\frac{T_{\rm UV}(r)}{s^{2}}\cdot\frac{s^{2}}{r^{2}+s^{2}}.$$

It does three jobs.

  1. Same UV behaviour. $\frac{s^{2}}{r^{2}+s^{2}}=1-\frac{r^{2}}{s^{2}}+\dots$, so $S=\frac{T_{\rm UV}}{s^{2}}+O(r^{0})$. The difference between the full integrand and $S$ is then $O(1/r)$ (odd in $\hat r$) plus $O(r^{0})$, which is integrable at $r=0$.
  2. Convergent at large $r$ without a phase. $T_{\rm UV}\sim\frac{1}{r^{2}}$, so $S\sim\frac{1}{r^{4}}$ and $\int d^{2}r\,S$ converges at $|r|\to\infty$. The only scale left in it is $s^{2}$.
  3. Trivial angular integral. Apart from its numerator, $S$ depends on $r$ only through $|r|$ (step 3).

Any $\frac{T_{\rm UV}}{r^{2}+a^{2}}$ would do. $a=|y-x|$ is chosen because it is the transverse scale already in the problem. A different $a$ moves $\pi\mathcal W C_{\rm UV}\log\frac{a^{2}}{s^{2}}$ between the UV term and the remainder, and the sum does not change.

2. Subtract and add

$$\int d^{2}r\;e^{iq\cdot r}\frac{T(r)}{\bar\xi D} =\underbrace{\mu^{-2\epsilon}\!\int d^{d}r\;S(r)}_{\text{UV, done in }d=2-2\epsilon} \;+\;\underbrace{\int d^{2}r\;\Big[e^{iq\cdot r}\frac{T(r)}{\bar\xi D}-\frac{T_{\rm UV}(r)}{r^{2}+s^{2}}\Big]_{d_{\perp}=2}}_{\equiv\ \widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}(\xi),\ \text{finite}}.$$

The factor $\mu^{-2\epsilon}$ restores the mass dimension, as before. The second integral is finite in $d=2$, so $d_{\perp}\to2$ there costs only $O(\epsilon)$.

Check that $\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}$ is UV finite. The angular average of the bracket on a small circle $|r|=\rho$, times $\rho^{2}$, must vanish if no $\frac{1}{r^{2}}$ is left. It does, and it falls like $\rho^{2}$:

3. Angular reduction of the counterterm (exact in $d$ dimensions)

The rule. For any function $f(r^{2})$,

$$\int d^{d}r\;r^{i}r^{m}f(r^{2})=\frac{\delta^{im}}{d}\int d^{d}r\;r^{2}f(r^{2}).$$

Why. The left side is a rank-2 tensor built from no external vector, so it must be $A\,\delta^{im}$. Taking the trace, $\delta^{ii}=d$, gives $A\,d=\int r^{2}f$.

Apply it to $S$. Take $f=\frac{1}{r^{4}(r^{2}+s^{2})}$ in the first term of $T_{\rm UV}$:

$$\int d^{d}r\,S=\Big[d_{\perp}\xi\bar\xi\,\frac{P^{i}s^{m}}{P^{2}}\,\frac{\delta^{im}}{d}+\Big(\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big)\frac{P\cdot s}{P^{2}}\Big] \int\frac{d^{d}r}{r^{2}(r^{2}+s^{2})} =\Big[\frac{d_{\perp}}{d}\,\xi\bar\xi+\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big]\frac{P\cdot s}{P^{2}}\int\frac{d^{d}r}{r^{2}(r^{2}+s^{2})}.$$

$d_{\perp}$ is the transverse dimension the vertices are contracted in. Here it is the same $d=2-2\epsilon$ as the integral: that was the convention behind the $\frac{d_{\perp}}{2}M_{0}=M_{0}+\pi$ step of the earlier PDF. So $\frac{d_{\perp}}{d}=1$ exactly, and

$$\int d^{d}r\,S=C_{\rm UV}(\xi)\,\frac{P\cdot s}{P^{2}}\int\frac{d^{d}r}{r^{2}(r^{2}+s^{2})} =C_{\rm UV}(\xi)\,\mathcal W\,s^{2}\int\frac{d^{d}r}{r^{2}(r^{2}+s^{2})}.$$
Difference from the momentum-space version. There, the phase $e^{iq\cdot r}$ singled out the direction $\hat q$. The tensor integral then had a traceless part $-\pi\hat q\hat q$, and the $d_{\perp}$ trap left the finite piece $\pi\xi\bar\xi\mathcal T$. Here nothing picks a direction, so neither piece appears in the UV term. The $\pi\xi\bar\xi\mathcal T$ has not been lost: it now sits inside $\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}$ (step 7.1).

4. The scalar master integral, step by step

$$J_{0}\equiv\mu^{-2\epsilon}\int\frac{d^{d}r}{r^{2}(r^{2}+s^{2})},\qquad d=2-2\epsilon.$$

(a) Radial form. Write $d^{d}r=r^{d-1}dr\,d\Omega_{d}$ with $\int d\Omega_{d}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}$. Put $u=r^{2}$, so $r^{d-1}dr=\frac{1}{2}u^{d/2-1}du$. For any $f$:

$$\int d^{d}r\,f(r^{2})=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\;u^{d/2-1}f(u).$$

(Check at $d=2$: $\pi\int du\,f(u)=\int_{0}^{\infty}2\pi r\,dr\,f(r^{2})$.)

(b) Our integrand. $f(u)=\frac{1}{u(u+s^{2})}$ gives $u^{d/2-1}f(u)=\frac{u^{a-1}}{u+c}$ with

$$a=\frac{d}{2}-1=-\epsilon,\qquad c=s^{2}.$$

(c) The Beta integral. Substitute $u=ct$:

$$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{a-1}}{u+c}\,du=c^{a-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{a-1}}{1+t}\,dt=c^{a-1}B(a,1-a)=c^{a-1}\,\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(1-a).$$

This uses $B(a,b)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{a-1}}{(1+t)^{a+b}}dt=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}$ with $b=1-a$, $\Gamma(1)=1$.

  • It converges for $0<a<1$, i.e. $-1<\epsilon<0$.
  • The small-$u$ (UV) end is what needs $a>0$, i.e. $\epsilon<0$. This is why the pole is written $\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}\equiv-\frac{1}{\epsilon}>0$.
  • The large-$u$ end converges for any $a<1$: the counterterm has no IR problem.

(d) Put it together. With $\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}=\frac{\pi^{1-\epsilon}}{\Gamma(1-\epsilon)}$:

$$J_{0}=\mu^{-2\epsilon}\,\frac{\pi^{1-\epsilon}}{\Gamma(1-\epsilon)}\,\Gamma(-\epsilon)\,\Gamma(1+\epsilon)\,(s^{2})^{-\epsilon-1} =\frac{\pi}{s^{2}}\;\big(\pi\mu^{2}s^{2}\big)^{-\epsilon}\;\Gamma(-\epsilon)\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\epsilon)}{\Gamma(1-\epsilon)}.$$

(e) Expand in $\epsilon$. Use $\Gamma(1+x)=1-\gamma_{E}x+O(x^{2})$. Term by term:

$$\Gamma(-\epsilon)=\frac{\Gamma(1-\epsilon)}{-\epsilon}=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}-\gamma_{E}+O(\epsilon),\qquad \frac{\Gamma(1+\epsilon)}{\Gamma(1-\epsilon)}=(1-\gamma_{E}\epsilon)(1-\gamma_{E}\epsilon)+O(\epsilon^{2})=1-2\gamma_{E}\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}),$$ $$\big(\pi\mu^{2}s^{2}\big)^{-\epsilon}=1-\epsilon\log\big(\pi\mu^{2}s^{2}\big)+O(\epsilon^{2}).$$

Multiply them out. Only the $\frac{1}{\epsilon}$ times the $O(\epsilon)$ terms give finite contributions:

$$\Big(-\frac{1}{\epsilon}-\gamma_{E}\Big)\big(1-2\gamma_{E}\epsilon\big)\big(1-\epsilon\log\pi\mu^{2}s^{2}\big) =-\frac{1}{\epsilon}-\gamma_{E}+2\gamma_{E}+\log\big(\pi\mu^{2}s^{2}\big)+O(\epsilon).$$ $$\Rightarrow\quad J_{0}=\frac{\pi}{s^{2}}\Big[-\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\gamma_{E}+\log\big(\pi\mu^{2}s^{2}\big)\Big]+O(\epsilon).$$

(f) $\overline{\rm MS}$. By definition $\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}=4\pi\mu^{2}e^{-\gamma_{E}}$, i.e. $\pi\mu^{2}=\frac{\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}e^{\gamma_{E}}}{4}$. So $\gamma_{E}+\log(\pi\mu^{2}s^{2})=\log\frac{\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}s^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}$, and:

$$J_{0}=\frac{\pi}{s^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}\,s^{2}\,e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}\Big],\qquad s^{2}=(y-x)^{2}.$$

Checks.

  • sympy's series of the exact $\Gamma$-function expression gives $\log(\pi\mu^{2}s^{2})+\gamma_{E}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}$.
  • The $\overline{\rm MS}$ rewriting differs from it by $0$.
  • With a hard cut-off $|r|>\rho$ in $d=2$, partial fractions $\frac{1}{u(u+c)}=\frac{1}{c}\big(\frac{1}{u}-\frac{1}{u+c}\big)$ give $\frac{\pi}{s^{2}}\log\frac{\rho^{2}+s^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\to\frac{\pi}{s^{2}}\log\frac{s^{2}}{\rho^{2}}$. This is the same $\log s^{2}$, with $\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}\leftrightarrow\log\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}$ + const.

5. The UV term of part (b)

Steps 3 and 4 together: $\mu^{-2\epsilon}\int d^{d}r\,S=C_{\rm UV}\,\mathcal W\,s^{2}\,J_{0}$, i.e.

$$\mu^{-2\epsilon}\int d^{d}r\;\frac{T_{\rm UV}(r)}{r^{2}+s^{2}}=\pi\,\mathcal W\,C_{\rm UV}(\xi)\Big[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}\,(y-x)^{2}\,e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}\Big].$$

Insert this into step 2. Pull one $\pi$ into the prefactor, so that $\frac{g^{4}}{16\pi^{5}}\to\frac{g^{4}}{16\pi^{4}}$, i.e. $\mathcal N$:

$$\frac{d^{3}N_{b}}{d^{3}k}=\mathcal N\!\int_{x,y,x',w'}\!e^{-ik\cdot(w'-x)}\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!d\xi\, \Big\{\mathcal W\,C_{\rm UV}(\xi)\Big[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}(y-x)^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}\Big] +\frac{1}{\pi}\,\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}(\xi)\Big\}\,\mathcal U_{b},$$ $$\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}(\xi)=\int d^{2}r\;\Big[e^{i\bar\xi k\cdot r}\frac{T(r)}{\bar\xi D}-\frac{T_{\rm UV}(r)}{r^{2}+s^{2}}\Big]_{d_{\perp}=2}.$$

This is the full $z$ integral of part (b). Nothing has been dropped.

6. The $p^{+}$ integral of the UV term

The bracket $\big[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\dots\big]$ does not depend on $\xi$. Before, it contained $\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi^{2}}$ through $q=\bar\xi k$; now it does not. So only the plain moment of $C_{\rm UV}$ is needed:

$$\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!d\xi\,C_{\rm UV}(\xi)=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{6}}_{\xi\bar\xi}+\underbrace{(L-1)}_{\xi/\bar\xi}+\underbrace{(L-1)}_{\bar\xi/\xi}=2L-\frac{11}{6}+O(\lambda).$$

Here $\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}d\xi=\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\big(\frac{1}{\bar\xi}-1\big)d\xi=\log\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}-(1-2\lambda)\to L-1$, and the same for $\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}$. The UV term of part (b) is therefore

$$\mathcal N\!\int_{x,y,x',w'}\!e^{-ik\cdot(w'-x)}\;\mathcal W\,\Big(2L-\frac{11}{6}\Big)\Big[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\mu^{2}_{\overline{\rm MS}}(y-x)^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}\Big]\;\mathcal U_{b}.$$

It contains no $L^{2}$ and no $\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-\frac{67}{18}$: those came from the $\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi^{2}}$, which is gone. They reappear in the remainder (§7).

7. The remainder $\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}$

7.1 Exact relation to the momentum-space remainder

The earlier remainder is $\mathcal I_{b}=\int d^{2}r\,e^{iq\cdot r}\big[\frac{T}{\bar\xi D}-\frac{T_{\rm UV}}{s^{2}}\big]$. The two remainders differ only in the subtraction term:

$$\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}-\mathcal I_{b}=\int d^{2}r\;T_{\rm UV}(r)\Big[\frac{e^{iq\cdot r}}{s^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}+s^{2}}\Big].$$

This is finite: at small $r$ the bracket is $O(r)$, and at large $r$ the first term oscillates while the second falls like $\frac{1}{r^{4}}$. So it can be done directly in $d=2$. Use polar coordinates with $\hat q$ along the first axis, $\hat r=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)$, and $x=\frac{|r|}{|s|}$.

Angular integrals (Jacobi–Anger: $\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,e^{ia\cos\phi}\cos n\phi=2\pi i^{n}J_{n}(a)$; the $\sin n\phi$ terms vanish by $\phi\to-\phi$):

  • Scalar: $\int d\phi\,e^{iq\cdot r}=2\pi J_{0}(qr)$.
  • Tensor: $\hat r^{i}\hat r^{m}=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{im}+\frac{1}{2}\cos2\phi\,(2\hat q^{i}\hat q^{m}-\delta^{im})+\frac{1}{2}\sin2\phi\,(\dots)$. So $\int d\phi\,e^{iq\cdot r}\hat r^{i}\hat r^{m}=\pi\delta^{im}J_{0}(qr)-\pi(2\hat q^{i}\hat q^{m}-\delta^{im})J_{2}(qr)$.
  • Without the phase: $\int d\phi\,\hat r^{i}\hat r^{m}=\pi\delta^{im}$.

Radial integrals.

$$\text{(R1)}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x}\Big[J_{0}(ax)-\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\Big]=-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{a^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4},\qquad \text{(R2)}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x}J_{2}(ax)=\frac{1}{2}.$$

Derivation of (R1). Insert $x^{\eta}$ and let $\eta\to0^{+}$ at the end. The two Mellin transforms are

$$\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\eta-1}J_{0}(ax)\,dx=a^{-\eta}\,2^{\eta-1}\frac{\Gamma(\eta/2)}{\Gamma(1-\eta/2)},\qquad \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\eta-1}}{1+x^{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{\eta}{2}\big)\Gamma\big(1-\tfrac{\eta}{2}\big).$$

With $\Gamma(\frac{\eta}{2})=\frac{2}{\eta}-\gamma_{E}+O(\eta)$ and $\Gamma(1\mp\frac{\eta}{2})=1\pm\frac{\gamma_{E}\eta}{2}$:

  • the first transform is $\frac{1}{\eta}-\gamma_{E}-\log\frac{a}{2}+O(\eta)$;
  • the second is $\frac{1}{\eta}+O(\eta)$.

Their difference is $-\gamma_{E}-\log\frac{a}{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{a^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}$.

(R2) is the standard $\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\nu}(t)\frac{dt}{t}=\frac{1}{\nu}$. It needs no subtraction, because $J_{2}\sim\frac{x^{2}}{8}$ at small $x$.

Assemble. Put $d^{2}r=s^{2}x\,dx\,d\phi$.

Scalar part, $\big(\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\big)\frac{P\cdot s}{P^{2}r^{2}}$:

$$\Big(\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big)\frac{P\cdot s}{P^{2}s^{2}}\,2\pi\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{dx}{x}\Big[J_{0}(q|s|x)-\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\Big] =-\pi\,\mathcal W\Big(\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}+\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\Big)\log\frac{q^{2}s^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}.$$

Tensor part, $2\xi\bar\xi\frac{P^{i}s^{m}}{P^{2}}\frac{\hat r^{i}\hat r^{m}}{r^{2}}$ at $d_{\perp}=2$:

$$\frac{2\xi\bar\xi}{P^{2}s^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{dx}{x}\Big\{\pi(P\cdot s)\Big[J_{0}-\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\Big]-\pi\big[2(P\cdot\hat q)(s\cdot\hat q)-P\cdot s\big]J_{2}\Big\} =-\pi\xi\bar\xi\,\mathcal W\log\frac{q^{2}s^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}+\pi\xi\bar\xi\,\mathcal T.$$

In the last step $\hat q=\hat k$, because $q=\bar\xi k$ with $\bar\xi>0$. Adding the two parts, and writing $\log q^{2}=\log\bar\xi^{2}+\log k^{2}$:

$$\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}(\xi)=\mathcal I_{b}(\xi)+\pi\xi\bar\xi\,\mathcal T-\pi\,\mathcal W\,C_{\rm UV}(\xi)\big[\log\bar\xi^{2}+\ell\big],\qquad \ell\equiv\log\frac{k^{2}(y-x)^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}.$$

Independent cross-check. Take the difference of the two dimensionally regulated counterterms: $\big(\pi\mathcal WC_{\rm UV}[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\mu^{2}}{q^{2}}]+\pi\xi\bar\xi\mathcal T\big)-\pi\mathcal WC_{\rm UV}[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\mu^{2}s^{2}e^{2\gamma_{E}}}{4}]$. The pole and $\mu$ cancel, and the result is exactly the line above. So the two counterterms have the same $\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}$ coefficient, as they must.

7.2 Behaviour at the endpoints in $\xi$

From the earlier PDF (§6.2, measured by 2D quadrature):

$$\mathcal I_{b}\xrightarrow{\xi\to0}\frac{\beta_{0}}{\xi},\qquad \mathcal I_{b}\xrightarrow{\xi\to1}\frac{-\pi\mathcal W\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}+\beta_{1}}{\bar\xi}.$$

The added terms, at each end:

  • $\xi\to0$: $C_{\rm UV}\to\frac{1}{\xi}$, $\log\bar\xi^{2}\to0$, $\xi\bar\xi\mathcal T\to0$. So they add $-\frac{\pi\mathcal W\ell}{\xi}$.
  • $\xi\to1$: $C_{\rm UV}\to\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$, $\log\bar\xi^{2}=-2\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$. So they add $\frac{2\pi\mathcal W\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}-\pi\mathcal W\ell}{\bar\xi}$.

Hence

$$\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}\xrightarrow{\xi\to0}\frac{\tilde\beta_{0}}{\xi},\qquad \widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}\xrightarrow{\xi\to1}\frac{+\pi\mathcal W\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}+\tilde\beta_{1}}{\bar\xi},\qquad \tilde\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}-\pi\mathcal W\ell,\quad\tilde\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}-\pi\mathcal W\ell.$$

The sign of the $\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$ flips, and this is expected. As §6.3 of the earlier PDF showed, the true integrand carries $+\frac{\pi\mathcal W}{\bar\xi}\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$, generated at large $|r|$ (between $|s|$ and $\frac{1}{q}$). The old counterterm ran out to $\frac{1}{q}$ and carried twice that. The new one stops at $|s|$ and carries none. So $\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}$ keeps exactly the log of the true integrand.

7.3 The plus prescription

At $\xi\to0$ it is a simple pole with a finite residue $\tilde\beta_{0}$, so the ordinary plus prescription works. At $\xi\to1$ there is a $\frac{\log}{\bar\xi}$, so two distributions are needed. Split

$$\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}=\underbrace{\Big[\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}-\frac{\tilde\beta_{0}}{\xi}-\frac{\tilde\beta_{1}+\pi\mathcal W\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}}{\bar\xi}\Big]}_{\text{integrable on }[0,1]} +\frac{\tilde\beta_{0}}{\xi}+\frac{\tilde\beta_{1}}{\bar\xi}+\pi\mathcal W\frac{\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}}{\bar\xi}.$$

Integrate each piece over $[\lambda,1-\lambda]$:

  • $\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\frac{d\xi}{\xi}=\log\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}=L+O(\lambda)$, and the same for $\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$.
  • With $v=\bar\xi$: $\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\frac{\log(1/v)}{v}dv=\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}v\Big]_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}\lambda-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(1-\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}L^{2}+O(\lambda^{2})$.
  • The bracket is integrable at both ends, so its limits can go to $0$ and $1$ at a cost of $O(\lambda)$.

In distribution language this is $\frac{1}{\xi}=[\frac{1}{\xi}]_{+}+\delta(\xi)L$, $\frac{1}{\bar\xi}=[\frac{1}{\bar\xi}]_{+}+\delta(\bar\xi)L$, $\frac{\log(1/\bar\xi)}{\bar\xi}=[\frac{\log(1/\bar\xi)}{\bar\xi}]_{+}+\frac{1}{2}\delta(\bar\xi)L^{2}$. Therefore

$$\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!d\xi\;\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}(\xi)=\big(\tilde\beta_{0}+\tilde\beta_{1}\big)L+\frac{\pi\mathcal W}{2}L^{2}+\widetilde{\mathcal F}_{b}+O(\Lambda),$$ $$\widetilde{\mathcal F}_{b}=\int_{0}^{1}\!d\xi\Big[\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}(\xi)-\frac{\tilde\beta_{0}}{\xi}-\frac{\tilde\beta_{1}+\pi\mathcal W\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}}{\bar\xi}\Big],\qquad \tilde\beta_{0}=\lim_{\xi\to0}\xi\,\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b},\qquad \tilde\beta_{1}=\lim_{\xi\to1}\Big[\bar\xi\,\widetilde{\mathcal I}_{b}-\pi\mathcal W\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}\Big].$$

$\widetilde{\mathcal F}_{b}$ in terms of the old $\mathcal F_{b}$. Subtract the two definitions and use 7.1, with the moments

  • $\int_{0}^{1}\big(C_{\rm UV}-\frac{1}{\xi}-\frac{1}{\bar\xi}\big)=\int_{0}^{1}(\xi\bar\xi-2)=\frac{1}{6}-2$;
  • $\int_{0}^{1}\big(C_{\rm UV}-\frac{1}{\bar\xi}\big)\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi^{2}}=\frac{5}{18}-2+\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-2$ (table in §6.1 of the earlier PDF).
$$\widetilde{\mathcal F}_{b}=\mathcal F_{b}+\pi\Big[\mathcal W\Big(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-\frac{67}{18}+\frac{11}{6}\,\ell\Big)+\frac{1}{6}\,\mathcal T\Big].$$
