Does the Row IV $1/\bar\epsilon$ feed the running coupling?

Four tests, and where $\beta_{0}$ actually lives

$\beta_{0}=\dfrac{11N_{c}}{3}-\dfrac{2n_{f}}{3}$,  $\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)=2N_{c}\Big[\dfrac{\xi}{[1-\xi]_{+}}+\dfrac{1-\xi}{\xi}+\xi(1-\xi)\Big] +\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{2}\delta(1-\xi)$,  $L_{r}=\log\big(4\pi^{2}\mu^{2}r^{2}\big)$

No — not this pole. The shape is deceptively close, so it is worth being careful, but all four tests come out the same way. A running-coupling term at $O(g^{4})$ must be $g^{4}\beta_{0}\log(\mu^{2}r^{2})\times(\text{LO})$: a pure $\delta(1-\xi)$, with no rapidity logarithm, from a ultraviolet pole, carrying $n_{f}$. Row IV's $\log(4\pi^{2}\mu^{2}r^{2})$ instead arrives multiplied by $\delta(1-\xi)\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}+\frac{\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}$, from a pole at $|z|\to\infty$, in a row with no quark loop. $\beta_{0}$ lives in the gluon self-energy / vertex rows and in the $g\to q\bar q$ row, none of which is this one.

1. Order counting: what an $O(g^{4})$ running-coupling term must look like

The LO cross section is $O(g^{2})$ and this row is $O(g^{4})$, so the only way a coupling can run inside this calculation is by absorbing a $\log\mu^{2}$ into the LO coupling:

$$g^{2}(\mu_{0})=g^{2}(\mu)\Big[1+\frac{\alpha_{s}\beta_{0}}{4\pi}\log\frac{\mu^{2}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}+\dots\Big] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \underbrace{g^{2}}_{\rm LO}\ \to\ \underbrace{g^{4}\,\beta_{0}\,\log(\mu^{2}r^{2})}_{O(g^{4})}\times(\text{LO structure}).$$

Read off the three properties that term must have. It is multiplied by the LO structure and nothing else — so a pure $\delta(1-\xi)$, no $\xi$-dependence, no $\mathcal P_{gg}$. It carries no rapidity logarithm — the LO cross section has no $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ to attach one to. And its coefficient is $\beta_{0}$.

Row IV's bracket is

$$\Big[\delta(1-\xi)\log\tfrac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}+\tfrac{\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}\Big] \Big[\tfrac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-\log\big(4\pi^{2}\mu^{2}r^{2}\big)\Big]\mathcal X,$$

and neither half fits:

  • The $\delta(1-\xi)$ half carries an extra $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$. That makes it $\bar\alpha_{s}\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}\,K\otimes(\text{LO})$ — the JIMWLK evolution term. Making the coupling in that kernel run is a correction to $\bar\alpha_{s}K$, i.e. $\bar\alpha_{s}^{2}\beta_{0}\log\times K\otimes(\text{LO})$, which is $O(g^{6})$ — one full order beyond this calculation. Running-coupling BK is a real and important effect, but it is not at this order.
  • The $\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)$ half is $\xi$-dependent. A $\beta$ function has no $\xi$; a splitting kernel does. They cannot be the same object.

2. The $\beta_{0}$ coefficient test: what $\frac{11N_{c}}{6}$ hangs off

It is worth seeing exactly where $\frac{11N_{c}}{6}$ comes from, because that is what tells you it is not here. Impose the momentum sum rule $\int_{0}^{1}\!dz\,z\,\mathcal P_{gg}(z)+2n_{f}\!\int_{0}^{1}\!dz\,z\,\mathcal P_{qg}(z)=0$ with $\mathcal P_{qg}=T_{R}[z^{2}+(1-z)^{2}]$:

piece$\int_{0}^{1}dz\;z\times$
$\dfrac{z}{[1-z]_{+}}$$-\tfrac32$
$\dfrac{1-z}{z}$$+\tfrac12$
$z(1-z)$$+\tfrac1{12}$
sum, $\times\,2N_{c}$$-\dfrac{11N_{c}}{6}$

so the $\delta(1-z)$ coefficient must be $+\frac{11N_{c}}{6}-\frac{2T_{R}n_{f}}{3} =\frac{11N_{c}}{6}-\frac{n_{f}}{3}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{2}$ (sympy, $T_{R}=\frac12$). Notice which entry carries it: the whole $\frac{11}{6}$ hangs off the $-\frac32$, i.e. off the soft endpoint $z\to1$ of $\frac{z}{[1-z]_{+}}$. Whatever regulates that endpoint decides what sits on $\delta(1-\xi)$.

In Row IV the soft endpoint is cut off in rapidity: the $p^{+}$ integral runs $\Lambda<p^{+}<\vee-k^{+}$, and the plus prescription returns $\delta(1-\xi)\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$. The constant $\frac{11N_{c}}{6}$ would require that endpoint to be cut off by transverse kinematics instead. So this row does not, and cannot, put $\beta_{0}$ on its $\delta(1-\xi)$ — it puts a rapidity logarithm there.

3. The $n_{f}$ test

$\beta_{0}=\frac{11N_{c}}{3}-\frac{2n_{f}}{3}$. The $n_{f}$ piece comes from the $g\to q\bar q$ row and from nowhere else. Row IV has no quark loop, so at best it could supply part of the $\frac{11N_{c}}{3}$ — and that partial sum is not a running coupling until the $-\frac{2n_{f}}{3}$ shows up beside it in the same $\log(\mu^{2}r^{2})$.

The one test that settles it, when you have more rows. Collect the coefficient of $\;\delta(1-\xi)\,\log(\mu^{2}r^{2})\,\mathcal X\,\mathcal U\;$ — with no $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ attached — from every row, including the quark-loop one. If the gluonic rows sum to $\frac{11N_{c}}{3}$ and the quark row supplies $-\frac{2n_{f}}{3}$, that combination is $\beta_{0}$ and it is absorbed by $\alpha_{s}(\mu)\to\alpha_{s}(1/r^{2})$ in the LO cross section. Row IV's contribution to that particular coefficient is zero, because its $\log(\mu^{2}r^{2})$ never appears without either $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ or $\mathcal P_{gg}(\xi)$ beside it.

4. The region test: a coupling is renormalised by a UV pole

This is the cleanest of the four. Coupling renormalisation absorbs a short-distance divergence. Row IV's pole is at long distance. From the power counting, with $u=y-z$, $|u|=\rho$:

$$\underbrace{\frac{u^{m}(u+r)^{m'}}{u^{2}(u+r)^{2}}\ \sim\ \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{1}{|r|},\quad d^{2}z=\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi \ \Longrightarrow\ \int_{0}\frac{d\rho}{|r|}\ \ \text{finite}}_{z\to y,\ \textbf{no UV pole}} \qquad \underbrace{\sim\frac{1}{z^{2}},\ d^{2}z=|z|d|z|d\phi\ \Longrightarrow\ \int^{R}\frac{d|z|}{|z|}=\log R}_{|z|\to\infty,\ \textbf{the pole}}$$

The single power of $u^{m}$ in the numerator is what kills the short-distance divergence. So there is no UV pole in $\mathcal G^{mm'}$ to renormalise anything with.

The last row is the reason the question is a good one: the colour and transverse structure of the two really do look alike, and $\log(\mu^{2}r^{2})\times(\text{LO structure})$ is exactly the fingerprint of $\alpha_{s}(1/r^{2})$. The five other rows are what separate them.

Consistency with what I said last time. If the $\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}$ cancels against the companion Group II rows, then $\log\mu^{2}$ cancels with it — they occur only in the single combination $\frac{1}{\bar\epsilon}-\log(4\pi^{2}\mu^{2}r^{2})$. So there is no leftover $\mu$-dependence in this row for a running coupling to absorb, which is the same conclusion reached from the other direction. These two readings agree; they are not alternatives.
