Transverse UV, and the $y'\to z$ region

Which divergence actually blocks the $+$ prescription

$r\equiv y'-z$,  $\xi\equiv p^{+}/k^{+}$,   ${\rm ph}\equiv e^{-i\xi k\cdot(y'-z)}$,  $\ell_{k}\equiv\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$

0. The short version

There is no transverse ultraviolet divergence — not at $y'\to z$, not anywhere. Your criterion is the right one, and at short distance it is satisfied.

But $y'\to z$ is still the region that matters, for a different reason: it is where the phase stops depending on $p^{+}$, so it is the source of the rapidity divergence. That is a longitudinal divergence living in a transverse region, not a transverse divergence — and the $+$ prescription handles it beautifully there (§2).

What blocks the split is at the other end: $|y'-z|\to\infty$. That divergence is infrared, not ultraviolet — and it blocks the split just as effectively, because the criterion is “no transverse divergence”, not “no transverse UV divergence”.

1. $y'\to z$: no ultraviolet divergence

At $y'=z$ only one of the four kernels is singular, $\frac{(y'-z)^{m}}{(y'-z)^{2}}$; the other three ($x'-y'$, $y-w$, $x-z$) are regular there. So the integrand is $O(1/\rho)$ and $\int\rho\,d\rho\,\rho^{-1}=\int d\rho$ converges. In fact it does better: the leading term integrates to zero by parity, $\int_{|r|<\epsilon}d^{2}r\,\frac{r^{m}}{r^{2}}=0$, so the small–disc integral is $O(\epsilon^{2})$. 2. Why $y'\to z$ is nevertheless the crucial region

It is where ${\rm ph}=e^{-i\xi k\cdot(y'-z)}\to1$, i.e. where the integrand stops knowing about $p^{+}$. The $\int\frac{d\xi}{\xi}$ is then completely unprotected, and that is exactly where the rapidity logarithm is generated. A longitudinal divergence localised in a transverse region is not a transverse divergence — and this one is what the $+$ prescription is built for.

Indeed the subtraction is doubly effective there. Expanding,

$$ {\rm ph}-1=-i\,\xi\,k\cdot(y'-z)+O(\rho^{2}),$$

so it supplies a factor $\xi$ — cancelling the $\frac1\xi$ pole — and a factor $\rho$, softening the kernel from $\rho^{-1}$ to $O(1)$. The numbers show it: the subtracted disc integral is $6.7\times$ smaller than the unsubtracted one at every $\epsilon$,

   eps=1e-02:  |T2 x ph| 3.938e-05   |T2 x (ph-1)| 5.870e-06
   eps=1e-03:  |T2 x ph| 3.938e-07   |T2 x (ph-1)| 5.870e-08
   eps=1e-04:  |T2 x ph| 3.938e-09   |T2 x (ph-1)| 5.870e-10

So at $y'\to z$ your reasoning is exactly right: no transverse divergence, and the $+$ prescription applies cleanly.

3. The obstruction is at the far end, and it is infrared

The region that fails is $|y'-z|\to\infty$, where the phase is the only thing suppressing the integrand and the subtraction removes it. Restating the measurement from the previous note, $dF/d\log R$ for $F(R)=\int_{|z|<R}d^{2}z$:

against $-2\pi=-6.28319$. So the criterion you are applying is the right one — it is just not satisfied, and the failure is at large distance rather than small.

Why this is structural, not accidental. In a dipole calculation the large–$z$ convergence is normally supplied by the kernel: the full BK kernel $\mathcal M=\frac{(x-y')^{2}}{(x-z)^{2}(y'-z)^{2}}$ falls as $\rho^{-4}$. Short–distance $z\to x$, $z\to y'$ is handled by the colour structure vanishing there. Here the short distance is fine for the reason in §1 — each kernel is single — but you have only the real piece $-2\mathcal K\sim\rho^{-2}$, so the large–$z$ end has nothing holding it up. Nor does the colour structure help: as $|z|\to\infty$, $U(z)\to\mathbb 1$ and the two brackets become

$$\delta^{bd'}\big[U^{e'c'}(y')-U^{c'e'}(x')\big]\rho^{e'}(x'),\qquad U^{ad}(y)\rho^{d}(y)\big[U^{be}(x)\rho^{e}(x)-\rho^{b}(x)\big],$$

which are $z$–independent, not zero. The coefficient of the logarithm survives.

4. On rewriting the logarithm

Yes, $\log\frac{\vee-k^{+}}{\Lambda}=\log\frac{\vee}{\Lambda}+\log\!\big(1-\frac{k^{+}}{\vee}\big)$ is exact, and the second piece is finite (and vanishes as $\vee\to\infty$). Same for splitting it as $\ell_{k}+\log\frac{\vee-k^{+}}{k^{+}}$. All of that is legitimate bookkeeping.

It does not help with the divergence, though, because the divergent object is the coefficient, not the logarithm. The second term is $\big(\text{transversely divergent }g(0)\big)\times\log[\cdots]$; rewriting the log as a sum of logs just distributes the same infinite coefficient over more terms. Only making $g(0)$ convergent does anything — and that means the virtual diagrams, which turn $-2\mathcal K$ into $\mathcal M$ and $\rho^{-2}$ into $\rho^{-4}$.

Where the rewriting does earn its keep is afterwards: once $g(0)$ is finite, choosing $\ell_{k}$ (via a $\theta(k^{+}-p^{+})$ subtraction) rather than $\log\frac{\vee-k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ gives each piece a finite $\vee\to\infty$ limit and matches the convention BK evolution expects.
