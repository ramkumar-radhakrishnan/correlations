3. Where, exactly, the $r$ integral can diverge

Before integrating, locate every dangerous point. There are three.

(a) $r\to0$, i.e. $z\to x$ — this is your UV. Every term of $T$ that survives the angular average at small $r$ behaves as $1/r^{2}$, and $\frac{1}{\bar\xi D}\to\frac{1}{\bar\xi D_{0}}=\frac{1}{s^{2}}$. In two dimensions $\int_{0}\frac{d^{2}r}{r^{2}}=2\pi\int_{0}\frac{d\rho}{\rho}$ is log divergent. This is a genuine short–distance divergence and is what §4–§6 regulate.

(b) $r\to s$, i.e. $z\to y$ — harmless. Three terms carry $\frac{1}{(s-r)^{2}}$, and in every one of them the numerator vanishes linearly there:

$$r\!\cdot\! s-r^{2}=r\!\cdot\!(s-r),\qquad s^{2}-s\!\cdot\! r=s\!\cdot\!(s-r),\qquad P\!\cdot\! s-P\!\cdot\! r=P\!\cdot\!(s-r).$$

So each behaves as $1/|s-r|$, and $\int d^{2}r\,|s-r|^{-1}$ converges. Note also that $D$ never vanishes: $D=(r-s)^{2}+M^{2}$ with $M^{2}=\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}s^{2}>0$. This is why the $\xi\to0$ endpoint, where $M^{2}\to0$, does not produce a second collinear pole.

(c) $r\to\infty$ — a real logarithm, and your note misses it. Expand $T$ at large $r$. The $\frac{1}{r^{2}}$ pieces all decay, but the third entry of (1.10),

$$\frac{P\cdot s}{2P^{2}s^{2}},$$

is $r$–independent. Hence $T(r)\to\frac{P\cdot s}{2P^{2}s^{2}}+O(1/r)$ and $\frac{T}{\bar\xi D}\to\frac{1}{\bar\xi r^{2}}\frac{P\cdot s}{2P^{2}s^{2}}$, giving another logarithm, cut off only by the phase at $r\sim1/q=1/(\bar\xi|k_{\perp}|)$. At generic $\xi$ this is a finite number; at $\xi\to1$ it is precisely what halves your $\log^{2}$ (§7).

5. The two Fourier masters in $d=2-2\epsilon$, from scratch

Start from the one identity everything else follows from. For $0<\alpha<d/2$,

$$\int d^{d}r\,\frac{e^{iq\cdot r}}{(r^{2})^{\alpha}} =\pi^{d/2}\,2^{\,d-2\alpha}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{d}{2}-\alpha\big)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,(q^{2})^{\alpha-d/2}.$$

Set $\alpha=1$, $d=2-2\epsilon$, and restore $\mu$ by the factor $\mu^{-2\epsilon}$ that makes the left side dimensionless:

$$\mu^{-2\epsilon}\!\int d^{d}r\,\frac{e^{iq\cdot r}}{r^{2}}=\pi^{1-\epsilon}2^{-2\epsilon}\Gamma(-\epsilon)\Big(\frac{q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\Big)^{\epsilon} =-\pi\Big[\frac1\epsilon+\gamma_{E}+\log\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\Big]+O(\epsilon) \;\equiv\;-\pi\,\mathcal L_{q},$$ $$\mathcal L_{q}\equiv\frac1\epsilon+\log\frac{q^{2}}{\bar\mu^{2}},\qquad \bar\mu^{2}=4\pi\mu^{2}e^{-\gamma_{E}}.$$

For the tensor master, differentiate rather than integrate. First, exactly in $d$ dimensions,

$$\partial^{m}\frac{r^{i}}{r^{2}}=\frac{\delta^{im}}{r^{2}}-\frac{2r^{i}r^{m}}{r^{4}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{r^{i}r^{m}}{r^{4}}=\frac12\Big[\frac{\delta^{im}}{r^{2}}-\partial^{m}\frac{r^{i}}{r^{2}}\Big].$$

Next, from $\partial_{q_{i}}e^{iq\cdot r}=ir^{i}e^{iq\cdot r}$ applied to the scalar master,

$$\int d^{d}r\,e^{iq\cdot r}\frac{r^{i}}{r^{2}}=-i\,\partial_{q_{i}}\Big[C_{1}(q^{2})^{\epsilon}\Big] =-2i\epsilon\,C_{1}\,q^{i}(q^{2})^{\epsilon-1}\ \xrightarrow{\ \epsilon\to0\ }\ \frac{2\pi i\,q^{i}}{q^{2}},$$

which is finite (the pole is killed by $\epsilon\Gamma(-\epsilon)=-\Gamma(1-\epsilon)$). Since $\int e^{iqr}\partial^{m}g=-iq^{m}\int e^{iqr}g$,

$$\int d^{d}r\,e^{iq\cdot r}\,\partial^{m}\frac{r^{i}}{r^{2}}=-iq^{m}\cdot\frac{2\pi i q^{i}}{q^{2}}=\frac{2\pi q^{i}q^{m}}{q^{2}},$$
$$\mu^{-2\epsilon}\!\int d^{d}r\,\frac{e^{iq\cdot r}}{r^{2}}=-\pi\mathcal L_{q}, \qquad \mu^{-2\epsilon}\!\int d^{d}r\,e^{iq\cdot r}\,\frac{r^{i}r^{m}}{r^{4}} =-\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal L_{q}\,\delta^{im}-\pi\,\hat q^{i}\hat q^{m}.$$
The $d_{\perp}$ trap — do not set $d_{\perp}=2$ before contracting. Trace the second master in $d$ dimensions: $-\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal L_{q}\,d-\pi=-\pi\mathcal L_{q}+\pi(\epsilon\mathcal L_{q})-\pi=-\pi\mathcal L_{q}$, using $\epsilon\mathcal L_{q}\to1$. It reproduces the scalar master only because the $-2\epsilon$ of $d_{\perp}$ eats the pole and leaves a finite $+\pi$. Your replacement $\frac{(P\cdot r)(s\cdot r)}{P^{2}r^{4}}\to\frac{s\cdot P}{d_{\perp}P^{2}r^{2}}$ (just above your 1.15) is correct for the pole and the $\log$, but it silently discards that $+\pi$ and the whole $\hat q^{i}\hat q^{m}$ structure — which is not proportional to $P\cdot s$ at all.

Why your (1.16)–(1.18) flips the pole. The convolution identity itself is fine: $\int d^{2}r\frac{e^{iqr}}{r^{2}}=(2\pi)^{2}\!\int\!\frac{d^{2}l}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{l\cdot(l-q)}{l^{2}(l-q)^{2}}$. But in $d$ dimensions $\int d^{d}r\,e^{iqr}\frac{r^{i}}{r^{2}}$ is $2i\pi^{1-\epsilon}2^{-2\epsilon}\Gamma(1-\epsilon)\frac{q^{i}}{(q^{2})^{1-\epsilon}}$, not $\frac{2\pi iq^{i}}{q^{2}}$; the $\epsilon$–dependent factors multiply a $\frac1\epsilon$ and therefore matter. On top of that, setting $\int\frac{d^{d}l}{l^{2}}=\int\frac{d^{d}l}{(l-q)^{2}}=0$ is the statement $\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm IR}}=0$, which is exactly the bookkeeping you are trying to track. The net effect is a pole of the wrong sign. A one–line sanity check settles it: with a cutoff, $\int_{\rho_{0}}d^{2}r\frac{e^{iqr}}{r^{2}}=2\pi\log\frac{2e^{-\gamma_{E}}}{q\rho_{0}}$ is large and positive as $\rho_{0}\to0$, so the pole must enter with the same sign as $\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{q^{2}}$: $$\mu^{-2\epsilon}\!\int d^{d}r\frac{e^{iqr}}{r^{2}}=\pi\Big[-\frac1\epsilon+\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{q^{2}}\Big] \;\equiv\;\pi\Big[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{q^{2}}\Big],\qquad \frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}\equiv-\frac1\epsilon .$$ So your expression (1.20) is the right one provided $\epsilon$ there means $\epsilon_{\rm UV}$, i.e. $d=2+2\epsilon_{\rm UV}$ — which is the correct sign of the analytic continuation for a position–space short–distance divergence anyway. Just say so, or the $\frac1\epsilon$ will fight the $\log$ when you combine rows.

6. The $r$ integration: subtract, integrate exactly, keep the rest finite

Your split $\frac1D=\frac{1}{D_{0}}+\big(\frac1D-\frac1{D_{0}}\big)$ is the right idea, but it only softens the $\frac{1}{r^{2}D}$ term; the $\frac{1}{r^{4}}$ tensor term is still divergent. Subtract the whole $r\to0$ asymptotics at once — nothing else changes and every step stays exact:

$$\int d^{d}r\,e^{iq\cdot r}\frac{T(r)}{\bar\xi D(r)} =\underbrace{\frac{1}{s^{2}}\int d^{d}r\,e^{iq\cdot r}\,T_{\rm UV}(r)}_{\text{carries the whole pole; done exactly}} +\underbrace{\int d^{2}r\;e^{iq\cdot r}\Big[\frac{T(r)}{\bar\xi D(r)}-\frac{T_{\rm UV}(r)}{s^{2}}\Big]}_{\displaystyle\equiv\ \mathcal I(\xi)\ \text{, finite at }\epsilon=0}$$

(using $\bar\xi D\to\bar\xi D_{0}=s^{2}$ at $r=0$). The subtraction works because, by construction, the bracket has no $r^{-2}$ even part left; what survives at small $r$ is $O(1/r)$ and odd, which is integrable in two dimensions. 7. The remainder $\mathcal I(\xi)$ is not uniformly finite — and this halves your $\log^{2}$

Your (1.21) says “$I(\xi)$ is a finite term” and then integrates only the explicit $\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{\bar\xi^{2}k^{2}}$ over $\xi$. $\mathcal I(\xi)$ is finite at every fixed $\xi$, but it blows up at the $\xi\to1$ endpoint faster than $\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$, and the $\xi$ integral sees that.

The $\bar\xi\to0$ end, analytically

Only one line of $T$ is $\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$–enhanced, namely (1.5), with $g(r)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}+\frac{r\cdot(s-r)}{2r^{2}(s-r)^{2}}$; and one line tends to a constant, namely the $\frac{P\cdot s}{2P^{2}s^{2}}$ of (1.10). Note that $\bar\xi D=\bar\xi(r-s)^{2}+\xi s^{2}$, so for $r\gg s$, with $u\equiv\bar\xi r^{2}/s^{2}$ and $g\to\frac{1}{2r^{2}}$,

$$\frac{T}{\bar\xi D}-\frac{T_{\rm UV}}{s^{2}}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{P\!\cdot\! s}{P^{2}s^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{1+u}\Big[\frac{1}{2u}+\frac12\Big]-\frac1u\right\}\frac{1}{s^{2}}\cdot s^{2} =-\frac{P\!\cdot\! s}{2P^{2}s^{2}}\,\frac{1}{\bar\xi\,r^{2}},$$

the $u$–dependence cancelling completely. Hence, with the phase cutting at $r\sim1/q=1/(\bar\xi|k_{\perp}|)$ and the expansion starting at $r\sim s$,

$$\mathcal I(\xi)\ \xrightarrow[\ \bar\xi\to0\ ]{}\ -\frac{\pi}{\bar\xi}\,\frac{P\!\cdot\! s}{P^{2}s^{2}}\, \log\frac{1}{\bar\xi\,|k_{\perp}|s}+\ \text{const}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \bar\xi\,\mathcal I\simeq-\pi\frac{P\!\cdot\! s}{P^{2}s^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}.$$

8. The $p^{+}$ integration, step by step

With $\lambda=\Lambda/k^{+}$ and $L=\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}=\log\frac1\lambda$. Three elementary integrals.

(i) The pole, and $\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{k^{2}}$

$$\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!\!d\xi\,\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}=\log\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}-(1-2\lambda)\to L-1, \qquad \int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!\!d\xi\,\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}\to L-1, \qquad \int_{0}^{1}\!d\xi\,\xi\bar\xi=\frac16,$$ $$\boxed{\ \int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!\!d\xi\;C_{\rm UV}(\xi)=2L-2+\frac16=2L-\frac{11}{6}\ }$$

— your (1.22), confirmed. (Numerically at $\lambda=10^{-6}$: $25.79768978$ against $25.79768778$.)

(ii) The $\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi^{2}}$ part of the explicit log

Split $\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{\bar\xi^{2}k^{2}}=\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{k^{2}}+2\log\frac1{\bar\xi}$. Term by term, with $u=\bar\xi$ where convenient:

$$-2\!\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!\frac{\xi}{\bar\xi}\log\bar\xi\,d\xi =-2\!\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!\Big(\frac{\log u}{u}-\log u\Big)du =-2\Big[\frac{\log^{2}u}{2}-u\log u+u\Big]_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\to L^{2}-2,$$ $$-2\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\bar\xi}{\xi}\log\bar\xi\,d\xi=-2\Big[\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log(1-\xi)}{\xi}d\xi}_{-\pi^{2}/6}-\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\log(1-\xi)d\xi}_{-1}\Big]=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-2,$$ $$-2\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\xi\bar\xi\log\bar\xi\,d\xi=-2\!\int_{0}^{1}\!(u-u^{2})\log u\,du=-2\Big(-\frac14+\frac19\Big)=\frac{5}{18}.$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad -2\!\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!\!C_{\rm UV}(\xi)\log\bar\xi\;d\xi = L^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-4+\frac{5}{18}=L^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-\frac{67}{18}.$$

(iii) The remainder

$$\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}\!\!\mathcal I(\xi)\,d\xi =\underbrace{-\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{P\!\cdot\! s}{P^{2}s^{2}}L^{2}}_{\text{from }\frac{1}{\bar\xi}\log\bar\xi} \;+\;\underbrace{\big(c_{0}+c_{1}\big)L}_{\text{from the }\frac{c_{0}}{\xi},\ \frac{c_{1}}{\bar\xi}\text{ ends}}\;+\;\text{finite},$$

10. Can you use the plus prescription for the $p^{+}$ integral?

The test is always the same one. Writing the $\xi$ integrand near an endpoint as $\frac{g(\xi)}{\xi}$,

$$\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{d\xi}{\xi}g(\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}d\xi\Big[\frac1\xi\Big]_{+}g(\xi)+g(0)\log\frac1\lambda$$

is legitimate if and only if $g(0)$ is finite. Apply it at both ends.

At $\xi\to0$ — yes. There $C_{\rm UV}\to\frac1\xi$, the explicit log tends to the finite $\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{k_{\perp}^{2}}$ (because $q=\bar\xi k\to k$), and $\xi\,\mathcal I(\xi)\to$ const (table in §7). So $g(0)$ exists, the $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ is a pure rapidity divergence, and $\big[\frac1\xi\big]_{+}$ is exactly the right bookkeeping: the $\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ goes into the JIMWLK/BK evolution of the target and the $+$–regulated remainder stays in the NLO impact factor.

At $\xi\to1$ — no. There $C_{\rm UV}\to\frac{1}{\bar\xi}$ but the accompanying function is $\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{\bar\xi^{2}k^{2}}+\bar\xi\mathcal I$, which diverges logarithmically as $\bar\xi\to0$. $g(0)$ does not exist, the naive $\big[\frac{1}{\bar\xi}\big]_{+}$ subtracts the wrong thing, and the leftover is the $\frac12\log^{2}$. That is not a defect of the prescription — it is the physical soft–collinear double log.

What to do instead at $\xi\to1$. Peel the log off first. Write the $\bar\xi\to0$ coefficient as $a\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}+b$ and use the two standard distributions

$$\frac{1}{\bar\xi}\ \to\ \Big[\frac{1}{\bar\xi}\Big]_{+}+\delta(\bar\xi)\log\frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \frac{\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}}{\bar\xi}\ \to\ \Big[\frac{\log\frac{1}{\bar\xi}}{\bar\xi}\Big]_{+} +\frac12\,\delta(\bar\xi)\log^{2}\frac{1}{\lambda},$$

so the $\Lambda$ dependence is carried entirely by the two $\delta$ terms and the rest is a genuine $+$–distribution acting on a finite function. This is the same conclusion as in the previous row: the single plus prescription is enough only where the companion function is regular, and here it is regular at exactly one of the two endpoints.

11. What the answer means

  • $-\frac{11}{6}$ is the beta function. $C_{\rm UV}=\frac{P_{gg}(\xi)}{2N_{c}}$ and $\int_{\lambda}^{1-\lambda}C_{\rm UV}=2L-\frac{11}{6}$, with $\frac{11}{6}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{2N_{c}}$, $\beta_{0}=\frac{11}{3}N_{c}$ (pure glue). Together with the $\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}+\log\frac{\bar\mu^{2}}{k_{\perp}^{2}}$ that multiplies it, this is precisely the combination absorbed by $g^{2}\to g^{2}(\mu)$. That is the “running coupling contribution” your note is named after, and it comes out of this row correctly.
  • No fragmentation function here. The Wilson lines sit at the parent positions $x,y,x',w'$ and there is no $\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}$ measure with the hard factor evaluated at $k_{\perp}/\xi$; only one gluon is measured and the second is integrated out. The $P_{gg}$ appears as the UV residue of a loop, not as a final–state collinear splitting. Consistent with what the two–$\mathfrak C$ row gave.
  • One thing to watch. The product $\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rm UV}}\times 2\log\frac{k^{+}}{\Lambda}$ is a mixed UV$\times$rapidity divergence. A term like that cannot survive on its own — the $\mu$ dependence of a rapidity–divergent piece has nothing to cancel against inside this row. It must cancel against the companion rows of Group I (or be absorbed together with them into the evolution of the coupling inside the JIMWLK kernel). Worth checking explicitly once the other rows are in the same scheme.
